\subsection{Total Cost of Ownership and Cloud Economics}\label{sec:cost}

\subsubsection{Model and Assumptions}\label{sec:cost:model}

The economic claim of this project --- that a serverless pipeline is materially
cheaper than provisioned infrastructure for this workload --- has to be
falsifiable, so the comparison is built as an explicit cost model rather than as
a single headline number. Both architectures are priced for the \emph{same}
traffic: 10{,}000 applications per month (20{,}000 uploaded images, two per
application) with a peak multiplier of ten times the baseline, in
\texttt{us-east-1}, on on-demand pricing, excluding tax.

Two choices of baseline deserve to be stated plainly, because they drive the
result.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The traditional side is sized for the peak, not the average.}
        That is what a bank that must survive a promotional campaign actually
        has to buy, and it is the assumption under which provisioning is
        expensive. It is also the assumption most favourable to the traditional
        architecture, since sizing for the average and accepting queueing at the
        peak would make it cheaper on paper and worse in service.
  \item \textbf{The traditional side is charged the same managed AI service as
        the serverless side would be, and we exclude it.} In practice a bank
        would also call a face-comparison service from its own servers. Because
        the traditional column in Table~\ref{tab:tco} omits that charge, the
        traditional baseline is slightly \emph{understated} and the reported
        saving is therefore conservative.
\end{itemize}

Unit prices were taken from the AWS Pricing Calculator~\cite{aws2026pricing} for the four line items
that dominate the result (EC2 \texttt{m5.large}, RDS \texttt{db.t3.medium},
Step~Functions state transitions and Rekognition \texttt{CompareFaces}) and
verified on 10 November 2026; the remaining line items are small enough that a
mis-estimate of twenty per cent moves the total by less than one dollar.

\subsubsection{Dimension-Level Comparison}\label{sec:cost:comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:tco} groups the two bills by cost dimension. The pattern is more
informative than the totals: compute, storage and database are exactly the
dimensions where provisioned capacity is paid for continuously and serverless
capacity is paid for per use, and they account for the whole of the difference.
The two dimensions that are \emph{not} improved by the architecture --- managed
AI inference and monitoring --- are small and are incurred either way.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption{Monthly cost by dimension at 10{,}000 applications per month (USD).}
  \label{tab:tco}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
    \toprule
    Cost dimension & Traditional & Serverless \\
    \midrule
    Compute                     & 140.16 &  0.63 \\
    Storage                     &  16.00 &  1.03 \\
    Database                    &  61.14 &  0.03 \\
    Messaging and orchestration &  15.18 &  3.11 \\
    AI inference                &     -- & 10.00 \\
    Monitoring                  &     -- &  1.00 \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Total}              & \textbf{232.48} & \textbf{15.80} \\
    \textbf{Saving}             & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{216.69 per month (93.2\%)}} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The saving is 93.2\%, well above the 70\% projected in the proposal. Two points
should be made about that gap. First, the proposal figure was an
order-of-magnitude projection made before any service had been priced; the model
reported here is built from verified unit prices and is therefore the number the
team is prepared to defend. Second, the saving is large here because the workload
is small and bursty. That is a property of the scenario, not a universal property
of serverless computing, and the next subsection shows where it stops being true.

\subsubsection{Sensitivity to Workload}\label{sec:cost:sensitivity}

A single comparison at 10{,}000 applications could be dismissed as a
conveniently chosen point, so the model is evaluated across three orders of
magnitude of traffic (Figure~\ref{fig:tco}). The two curves have different
shapes, and the shapes are the argument: the traditional bill is essentially
flat, because the servers run whether or not anyone applies, while the
serverless bill is essentially linear in the number of applications.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/fig_f_tco.pdf}
  \caption{Monthly cost against workload. Provisioned capacity is flat; serverless
           cost scales with usage. The advantage is largest at low and bursty
           volume and narrows as traffic grows.}
  \label{fig:tco}
\end{figure}

Three regimes are visible. At 1{,}000 applications per month the serverless bill
is under two dollars and the saving is 99.3\%. At the modelled baseline of
10{,}000 it is 15.80 against 232.48, a saving of 93.2\%. At 100{,}000
applications the serverless bill reaches roughly 158 dollars while the
traditional bill is unchanged, and the saving falls to about 32\%. Extrapolating
the same line, the serverless cost overtakes the traditional cost at a little
under 150{,}000 applications per month.

That crossover is an honest finding rather than a defect, and it is worth stating
explicitly in a report that is arguing \emph{for} serverless: at sustained high
volume the linear cost of per-request billing eventually exceeds the fixed cost
of capacity that is now fully utilised. The conclusion of this section is
therefore conditional --- serverless wins decisively for the bursty, low-to-moderate
volume profile that a retail onboarding campaign actually produces, and a bank at
a different operating point should re-run the model rather than adopt this
architecture by default.

\subsubsection{Measured Cost}\label{sec:cost:measured}

The figures above are a forecast. To test it, the load test of
Section~\ref{sec:testing} is used to derive an empirical cost per application: the
number of Lambda invocations, Step~Functions executions and S3 PUTs actually
observed are multiplied by the same verified unit prices, giving a cost for 100
applications that is then scaled to 10{,}000. Because a hundred applications cost
well under a dollar, the meaningful quantity is the per-application cost, not the
absolute spend of a single test run, and it is that per-application figure which
is compared against the model of Table~\ref{tab:tco}. A material disagreement
between the two would indicate that one of the workload assumptions --- image
size, Lambda duration, or transitions per execution --- was wrong, and would be
reported as a correction rather than smoothed over. One caveat applies to the
comparison: the load test deliberately mixes corrupt files into the traffic, and
those never reach the AI stage, so the measured per-application cost is expected
to sit slightly \emph{below} the model's. The two are therefore compared on the
valid applications only.

\subsubsection{Limitations}\label{sec:cost:limitations}

Three limitations bound the result. The model prices infrastructure only and
excludes engineering labour and compliance staffing; since a provisioned stack
needs materially more of both, excluding them biases the comparison \emph{against}
serverless, which is the safe direction for our claim. Prices were read on a
single date and in a single region, and AWS pricing changes. Finally, the
traditional baseline is a representative small deployment rather than a
quotation: a bank with an existing data centre and depreciated hardware would
report a lower marginal cost than the model assumes, although its \emph{marginal}
cost for an incremental marketing campaign would still be dominated by the extra
capacity the campaign requires. The economic case for elasticity rests on that
marginal decision, which is why the burst multiplier was kept in the model rather
than being averaged away.
